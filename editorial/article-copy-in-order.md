## 2. Introduction

In 1939, physicist Ernest Lawrence won Berkeley’s first Nobel prize for his invention of the cyclotron, a machine that accelerated charged particles and introduced a new way to study the atom. Nearly a century later, Berkeley’s Nobel laureates have investigated how cells transport proteins, developed theories for economic equilibrium, and theorized that the universe is expanding at an increasing rate. Their work spans decades of research across campus and at the Lawrence Berkeley National Lab.

As this year’s Nobel Prize announcements began, The Daily Californian examined the history of Berkeley’s laureates, tracing the recipients and their connections to the university. While the university primarily recognizes 63 Berkeley Nobel Laureates, over 100 Laureates have been associated with Berkeley as alumni, short-term faculty or researchers.

“Our list includes alumni, faculty, emeriti and long-term postdoctoral researchers whose education and careers are rooted here. We expect to add many more Berkeley names in the years ahead,” said Will Kane, a spokesperson for UC Berkeley.

The university’s list includes Laureates holding a degree from Berkeley, holding a multi-year postdoc or fellowship at Berkeley, or if they are current faculty, emeriti, or faculty who stayed until retirement. Other affiliations, including shorter research appointments or visiting lecturers, generally fall outside this criteria.

## 4. Interview and conclusion

Berkeley’s 63 Nobel Laureates represent 7% of all Nobel Laureates in Chemistry, Physics, Medicine, Literature and Economics.

For Randy Schekman, who won the 2013 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine, Berkeley’s research culture has been central to his career. He recalled the “special cozy feeling” he experienced when first visiting the university in 1975.

“We don't have the deep pockets of a place like Harvard, Yale, Princeton, or Stanford. So we have to be interdependent in a way that is much less common. And we are all keenly interested in applying our work,” Schekman said.

However, Sheckman is worried about the future of research amidst funding cuts and the political climate.

“We admit many fewer graduate students because faculty can't afford them. Over the next two years, people are going to be closing their labs,” he said.

Still, Schekman emphasized the importance of preserving curiosity-driven research. He pointed to Jennifer Doudna’s Nobel-Prize winning research on CRISPR, which was built on decades of previous discoveries.

"I think my concern going forward is that we don't lose sight of that investment in basic science," Schekman said.

As Nobel prizes are announced throughout the week, Berkeley will be preparing for additions to its list of Laureates.

### Lawrence Lab — existing copy

Founded by Ernest Lawrence in 1931 as the Radiation Laboratory, or “Rad Lab.” The lab was named after Lawrence in 1959, and, in 1995, finally renamed to its current title, Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory, a U.S. Department of Energy lab managed by University of California.

### Manhattan Project — NEW copy written in the previous pass

The Manhattan Project was the United States’ World War II program to develop the first atomic bombs. Berkeley researchers contributed through uranium-isotope separation and work at Los Alamos, where J. Robert Oppenheimer directed the laboratory.

### Ernest Lawrence

1939 · Physics

Faculty

Ernest Lawrence was the first UC Berkeley faculty member to receive a Nobel Prize. Lawrence joined Berkeley faculty in 1928 as an Associate Professor in Physics and became the youngest professor at the school. In 1929, he invented the cyclotron, winning him the Nobel Prize in Physics. The cyclotron accelerated particles to very high velocities without the use of high voltages, allowing for the discovery of radioactive isotopes. In 1931, Lawrence co-founded then-called Radiation Laboratory (Rad Lab), now the Lawrence Berkeley National Lab, which has hosted a number of future Nobel Laureates.

Nobel Prize record: https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/physics/1939/lawrence/facts/

### John Clarke

2025 · Physics

Faculty

After completing a postdoctoral fellowship at UC Berkeley, John Clarke joined the Physics faculty and became a researcher at Lawrence Berkeley National Lab in 1969. He received the 2025 Nobel Prize in Physics with Michel H. Devoret and John M. Martinis for discovering quantum mechanical properties on a macroscopic scale. Clarke is now a Professor Emeritus of the Graduate School in the Department of Physics.

Nobel Prize record: https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/physics/2025/summary/

### Omar Yaghi

2025 · Chemistry

Faculty

Omar M. Yaghi was appointed the James and Neeltje Tretter Chair Professor of Chemistry at Berkeley, and also serves as the Founding Director of the Berkeley Global Science Institute. Yaghi received the 2025 Nobel Prize in Chemistry with Richard Robson and Susumu Kitagawa for their development of metal-organic frameworks. In July, Yaghi announced his leave from Berkeley to Tsinghua University, where he will lead an AI-driven materials research centre.

Nobel Prize record: https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/chemistry/2025/summary/
